Is Paying More for College Really Worth It?

Comparing tuition, net price, and graduate earnings across selected U.S. public universities.

The main question

Does paying more for college actually mean earning more after graduation?

When students choose a university, tuition is often one of the first things they look at. But a higher tuition price does not necessarily mean better earnings after graduation.

For this project, I used College Scorecard data to compare college costs and graduate earnings. After receiving feedback from Part I, I also added average net price because tuition alone does not show the full cost of attending college.

I wanted to see what the numbers actually tell us before deciding whether a higher price is worth it. The charts are a starting point, not a ranking of which university is best.

VISUALIZATION 02   /   LOOKING BEYOND TUITION

What changes when we compare net price?

Tuition is not the whole cost of college. Students also need to consider housing, meals, books, and other expenses. Average net price considers the overall cost of attendance after grants and scholarships. I added this chart to see whether the relationship with earnings looks different.

Tuition

The published tuition and required fees. It does not include the full cost of living and attending college.

Average net price

The average cost of attendance after grants and scholarships for eligible students. It is not what every student personally pays.

CONCLUSION   /   MAIN TAKEAWAY

College value is more than the tuition price

A high tuition price by itself tells us very little about what a student may get back financially. I would compare net price, earnings outcomes, financial aid, academic major, and location together instead of judging schools by tuition alone.

A question for students

When comparing two schools, check the estimated net price for your situation first. Then look at earnings data for relevant student groups and think about your major, location, and financial aid.

Limits of my draft data

The 30 universities were selected across different states and tuition levels, not randomly. This is an exploratory sample, so the results do not represent all U.S. universities. Tuition is an annual published price, while the earnings data describe an eligible group of former students from a different time period. This is not an exact return-on-investment (ROI) calculation, and a relationship in these charts does not mean tuition caused higher earnings. I also need to verify unusual net-price values before Part III.

02 / User research

What did classmates think?

In our Part II class critique, three classmates looked through my Tableau drafts and gave suggestions about the sample, chart design, and story. I used their comments to plan the changes below.

Target audience

My intended readers are students and families choosing a college. For this critique, I worked with classmates who have personal experience with university decisions, although they are not a representative sample of all prospective students or parents.

Research approach

I showed my three Tableau drafts during our in-class group critique. We talked about which parts were unclear and what information should come next. I summarized the discussion by issue rather than assigning comments to a single speaker.

Interview findings

Our group raised five main issues. These are my notes from the discussion, not direct quotes or separate one-on-one interviews.

01 / Data selection

Why these 30 universities?

Some classmates thought many universities were unfamiliar and questioned how they were selected. They were unsure whether the sample could represent U.S. universities.

Next: explain selection and consider a larger, more systematic sample.
02 / Scatter plots

Does the straight line fit the points?

Some classmates felt that the dots were too scattered and that the trend lines might make the relationships look stronger than they are.

Next: check model fit and explain or remove the trend lines.
03 / Story logic

Why did net price appear?

My original question focused on tuition and earnings, so some classmates did not understand why the second chart suddenly introduced net price.

Next: explain tuition versus net price before the chart.
04 / Colors

Two colors hide the other groups

After I changed the bar chart from five colors to two, most universities looked the same. Classmates wanted to see clearer differences across tuition groups.

Next: try five shades of one color for Q1–Q5.
05 / A stronger introduction

Show how college costs changed over time

Classmates suggested an additional chart showing historical college costs. They shared examples of average college costs over time and tuition growth compared with wage growth. The second idea is useful as context, although general wage growth is not the same as graduate earnings.

Next: look for historical data and consider a simple opening chart.
Research synthesis

After we discussed the charts, I could see two problems I had overlooked. I had not explained why I chose these particular universities, and I had left the trend lines in without showing how much the dots vary. I also thought switching the bar chart to two colors would make it easier to read, but the group felt it hid differences between tuition bands. Before adding more charts, I need to fix those explanations and design choices. The historical-cost suggestion may help the introduction in Part III.

03 / Identified changes for Part III

What I will improve next

After Part I, I already added average net price and simplified the bar chart colors. The new feedback helped me see what still needs work.

What I learned My plan for Part III
The 30-university sample is not very clear. Explain the selection process and consider a larger or more systematic College Scorecard sample.
The trend lines may not represent the scattered points well. Check the strength of each relationship and consider removing the lines if they are misleading.
The purpose of net price is unclear. Add a simple explanation of tuition, net price, and financial aid before the second chart.
Two colors make the tuition groups hard to compare. Test five shades of the same color for Q1 to Q5 and keep the labels easy to read.
The story needs more historical context and another data source. Explore NCES historical college cost data (Digest Table 330.10), and consider adding one opening line chart or bar chart. Check whether amounts are adjusted for inflation before comparing years.
Tuition growth versus wage growth could be interesting. Check whether comparable wage data are available; use them only as background, not as a substitute for graduate earnings.
The main question needs a clearer takeaway. Give readers practical factors to compare rather than suggesting one university is best for everyone.

Next step

My first priority is to review the 30-school sample and check the trend lines. After that, I will revise the net-price explanation and tuition-band colors. If I find a reliable historical series, I may use one additional chart to set up the story.
