Telling Stories with Data / Final Project Part II

Is Paying More for College Really Worth It?

Comparing Tuition, Net Price, and Graduate Earnings Across U.S. Public Universities

The Main Question

Does paying more for college actually mean earning more after graduation?

When students choose a university, tuition is often one of the first things they consider. However, a higher tuition price does not necessarily mean better earnings after graduation.

For this project, I used College Scorecard data to compare college costs and graduate earnings. After receiving feedback from Part I, I also added average net price because tuition alone does not show the full cost of attending college.

My goal is to help students and their families understand what makes a college worth its cost, rather than assuming that a more expensive university must be better.

What Changes When We Compare Net Price?

Tuition is not the whole cost of college. Students also need to consider housing, meals, books, and other expenses. Average net price considers the broader cost of attendance after grants and scholarships. I added this chart to see whether the relationship with graduate earnings looks different.

05 / MAIN TAKEAWAY

College Value Is More Than the Tuition Price

The purpose of my project is not to tell students which university they should choose. Instead, I want to show why comparing tuition alone may not be enough. Students should also consider net price, graduate earnings, financial aid, academic major, and location.

A Question for Students

Before choosing a college, compare what it may cost you, what students from that school go on to earn, and what other factors matter to your own goals.

Limitations of My Current Data

The 30 universities were selected to cover different states and tuition levels, not randomly. They do not represent all U.S. universities. The earnings data cover an eligible group of former students rather than every graduate. These charts show associations rather than cause and effect.

02 / User Research

What Did My Classmates Think?

For Part II, I discussed my draft visualizations with three classmates from Part II Critique Group 8. I wanted to understand whether the charts were clear, whether the story made sense, and what I should improve.

Target Audience

My main audience is prospective college students and their families. I asked classmates with experience choosing a university to review the charts because they could relate to this decision.

Research Approach

I showed my three Tableau visualizations during our class critique session. We discussed data selection, the meaning of the charts, net price, colors, and possible improvements. The suggestions were developed through our group discussion.

During our group discussion, we identified five main areas that could be improved. The following table organizes these discussion themes under the three group members in a 2–2–1 format. This is an organizational grouping for the report, not a record of who originally suggested each point.

Group Member Discussion Topics Main Feedback
Anita Huang University Selection The 30 selected universities were not very familiar to the group, and the selection process was unclear.
Anita Huang Trend Lines The points in the scatter plots were quite spread out, so the trend lines did not seem to represent them very well.
Wenjia Jian Net Price The purpose of adding net price was not clear when the original topic focused on tuition and earnings.

Main Feedback and What I Learned

01 / University Selection

Why These 30 Universities?

Some classmates thought the selected universities were unfamiliar and questioned whether they could represent universities across the United States.

Next: Explain the selection process and consider a larger or more systematic sample.
02 / Trend Lines

Do the Trend Lines Fit the Data?

The dots in both scatter plots were quite spread out. Some classmates felt that the straight lines might make the relationships look stronger than they really are.

Next: Check the relationship strength and consider removing misleading trend lines.
03 / Net Price

Why Did I Include Net Price?

Some classmates did not understand why I introduced average net price when my original research question focused on tuition and graduate earnings.

Next: Explain tuition and net price more clearly before the second chart.
04 / Chart Colors

Two Colors Hide the Other Groups

I originally used five colors for the tuition groups but changed them to two colors. The feedback showed that most universities then looked the same, making it harder to compare the groups.

Next: Try five shades of the same color for Q1 to Q5.
05 / Historical Trends

Show How College Costs Have Changed Over Time

Classmates suggested adding another chart to provide more background for my project. They showed me two examples. The first compared average college costs across different years. The second compared university tuition growth with wage growth over time. I think this is useful because my current visualizations only compare universities at one point in time.

Next: Explore historical college cost data and consider adding a simple line chart or bar chart in the introduction.
Research Synthesis

The feedback helped me realize that some problems are related to my data, while others are related to how I present the information. The comments about university selection and trend lines made me think more carefully about how reliable my analysis is. The comments about net price, colors, and historical context showed me that I need to explain my story more clearly. Overall, I learned that having more charts does not automatically make a better data story. Each chart should have a clear purpose and help readers understand the main question.

03 / Identified Changes for Part III

What I Will Improve Next

Based on the feedback from Part I, I already added average net price and simplified the colors of my bar chart. The new feedback helped me understand what still needs improvement.

Research Synthesis Anticipated Changes for Part III
The selection of 30 universities is unclear and may not be representative. Explain the selection process and consider a larger or more systematic sample from College Scorecard.
The trend lines may not fit the scattered points very well. Check the relationship strength and consider removing the trend lines if they are misleading.
Some readers do not understand why net price is included. Add a short explanation of tuition, net price, and financial aid before the second visualization.
Two colors make tuition groups difficult to compare. Test five shades of the same color for Q1 to Q5 while keeping the chart easy to read.
The story needs more historical context and another data source. Explore historical college cost data from NCES or College Board. Consider adding an opening historical chart and check whether data are adjusted for inflation.
Tuition growth compared with wage growth could be interesting. Check whether suitable historical wage data are available. Use this comparison as background, not as a replacement for graduate earnings.
The overall story needs a clearer takeaway. Focus more on helping students understand what information they should consider when comparing universities.

Next Step

For Part III, I want to improve my data selection first, then the chart design and explanations. My goal is to create a story that readers can follow without needing extra explanations from me.
